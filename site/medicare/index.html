Why does Medicare spend more in some counties than others?

An analysis of standardized Medicare spending per beneficiary across about 3,000 US counties, 2014 to 2024, using SQL (DuckDB) and Python.

  • $620less per beneficiary in rural counties than in similar metro counties, after controlling for income, poverty, age, and Medicaid enrollment
  • 10×the national average on skilled nursing care in Miller County, GA, the highest-spending county in 2024

Where spending is high

Medicare does not spend the same amount on everyone. In 2024 the middle 90% of counties ranged from about $9,500 to $15,500 per beneficiary, even after removing differences in local prices. Spending is highest in Florida, Oklahoma, and the Gulf South, and lowest in the Pacific Northwest, the northern Mountain West, northern New England, Alaska, and Hawaii.

Richer counties do not spend more

A natural guess is that wealthier places use more care. The data show the opposite, at least weakly: higher-income counties spend slightly less per beneficiary, and the pattern is stronger among rural counties. Poorer counties have more beneficiaries who also qualify for Medicaid ("dual-eligible"), a group that tends to need more care.

Metro and rural counties grew at the same pace

Metro counties spend more per beneficiary than rural ones every year, but the gap has been stable. Both grew about 37% between 2014 and 2024, with a dip in 2020 when the use of care fell during COVID-19.

What explains the differences?

I regressed county spending per beneficiary on income, poverty, rurality, average age, the share of beneficiaries also on Medicaid, and the share of Medicare enrollees in Medicare Advantage, with year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by state. As a robustness check, I re-ran the model weighting each county by its number of beneficiaries.

  • Rurality matters once other factors are held constant. In the raw data, the typical metro and rural county spend almost the same. After accounting for rural counties' higher poverty and Medicaid enrollment, rural counties spend about $600 to $850 less per beneficiary.
  • Poverty, not income, carries the signal. Income and poverty are closely related (correlation of about −0.74). With both in the model, poverty is significant and income is not.
  • The results are not driven by small counties. Weighting by beneficiaries keeps every sign and significance level and makes the rural gap larger. Dropping Miller County changes nothing meaningful.

These are associations across counties, not causal effects. Standardized spending removes differences in prices, not in health, so part of the rural gap may reflect differences in health or in access to care.

Sidebar: Miller County, Georgia

Miller County, a rural county in southwest Georgia, had the highest standardized spending of any county in the sample in 2024: $29,853 per beneficiary, more than double the national average of $12,553. Its spending rose from about $11,000 in 2014, with most of the increase coming after 2021.

The largest single difference is skilled nursing facility care: $8,691 per beneficiary, about 10 times the national $874. Miller is above the national average in most other categories too, including inpatient and outpatient hospital care.

Data and methods

Data

  • Medicare spending: CMS Medicare Geographic Variation Public Use File, 2014 to 2024, county level. The outcome is standardized spending per Original Medicare beneficiary, which removes geographic differences in Medicare payment rates.
  • Income and poverty: US Census Bureau Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates (SAIPE), pulled from the Census API.
  • Rurality: USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, 2023. Codes 1 to 3 are metro; 4 to 9 are rural (nonmetro).

Sample

Counties with fewer than 500 Original Medicare beneficiaries are excluded because their averages are noisy (the smallest county in the data had 14). US territories are excluded. The final panel has 32,699 county-years across 3,011 counties. More than 99.7% of rows match to the income and rurality data; the exceptions are Connecticut before its 2022 switch to planning regions and one Alaska area that was split in 2019.

Tools

DuckDB (SQL) for loading, reshaping, and joining; pandas and statsmodels for the regressions; Plotly for the charts.
